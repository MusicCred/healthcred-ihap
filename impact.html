Bipartisan Congressional Support

This issue has no party.

Legislation supporting pre-release healthcare enrollment has attracted support from Democrats and Republicans alike — because the data is the same regardless of who's reading it.

📜
Bipartisan Reentry Act (2025)
Reintroduced April 2025 by Reps. Tonko (D-NY), Turner (R-OH), Pettersen (D-CO), and Rutherford (R-FL) — a former sheriff representing Florida's 5th District. Empowers states to restore Medicaid access up to 30 days before release.
⚕️
Medicaid Reentry Act
Led by Senators Baldwin (D-WI) and Braun (R-IN) in the Senate. Specifically addresses pre-release Medicaid activation so coverage is ready the moment someone walks out — not 30 days later.
🔄
Second Chance Reauthorization Act (2024)
Backed by Senators Durbin (D-IL), Capito (R-WV), Booker (D-NJ), Cornyn (R-TX), and Welch (D-VT). Reauthorizes critical grant programs for housing, career training, and treatment. Has reduced recidivism 23% nationally since 2008.

In 2025, a bipartisan group of 88 lawmakers signed a joint letter calling for continued Second Chance Act funding — led by Reps. Carol Miller (R-WV) and Danny Davis (D-IL). The message was clear: this works, and it needs to keep going.

The Research

Numbers that don't move, no matter who's counting.

Every major statistic IHAP cites comes from peer-reviewed research, federal agency reports, or published government data.

12.7×
Higher risk of death in the first two weeks after release
Drug overdose is the leading cause. People walk out with untreated conditions, no medication, and nowhere to turn. This isn't a failure of character — it's a failure of infrastructure.
Source: The Lancet
16%
Reduction in recidivism with Medicaid enrollment at release
Among formerly incarcerated individuals with severe mental illness, Medicaid enrollment upon release was directly associated with a 16% reduction in recidivism. Studies conducted in Florida and Washington State.
Source: U.S. Dept. of Health & Human Services, ASPE Report to Congress — January 2023
60%
Lower odds of returning to jail when insured within 30 days of release
Insurance within the first month isn't just helpful — it's transformative. People with coverage in that window are 60% less likely to come back. This is why IHAP starts at intake.
Source: Peer-reviewed research on reentry health outcomes
30%
Reduction in violent crime recidivism with Medicaid access
Medicaid access is linked to a 30% reduction in violent crime recidivism and a 24% reduction in public-order crime reoffenses. Safer communities aren't a side effect of good healthcare policy — they're the direct result.
Source: Peer-reviewed research on Medicaid and criminal justice outcomes
Case Study

Lee County, Florida: $5.9 million in year one.

Lee County implemented IHAP's HealthCred enrollment program and documented $5.9 million in savings within its first year of operation. When people have healthcare, they use doctors instead of emergency rooms. They manage chronic conditions before they become crises. They don't come back — and each person who doesn't come back saves the county tens of thousands of dollars.

The Ripple Effect

When one person gets care and stays out, everyone wins.

🏘️
Measurably Safer Communities
A 30% reduction in violent crime recidivism. A 24% reduction in public-order reoffenses. These aren't projections — they're published results from studies on what happens when Medicaid enrollment meets reentry. IHAP creates the coverage and care continuity that drives those numbers.
👨‍👩‍👧
Families Kept Together
10 million children in the U.S. have a parent who has been incarcerated. Every person who stays out is a parent present, a partner supported, a sibling no longer absent. The ripple of one person getting care reaches every person connected to them.
💰
Enormous Savings for Taxpayers
One year in county jail costs $45,000–$53,000 per person. People with justice involvement make up 4.2% of the U.S. population but account for 8.5% of all ER spending — an estimated $5.2 billion in excess costs annually. Prevention is always less expensive than crisis.
